\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

% Every literature claim cites a source verified in references.bib (see paper/README.md).

Molecular docking predicts how a small molecule binds to a protein of known structure and
is a standard tool of structure-based drug design, both for proposing binding modes and for
virtual screening \citep{kitchen2004docking,irwin2016docking}. Screens of hundreds of
millions of molecules have found new chemotypes for well-studied targets
\citep{lyu2019ultralarge}, and published protocols now guide such campaigns
\citep{bender2021practical}. Classical engines with empirical scoring functions, such as
AutoDock Vina \citep{trott2010vina,eberhardt2021vina}, remain the workhorses of this work.
Deep-learning methods have recently been proposed as alternatives, but the PoseBusters study
found that many of them produce physically invalid poses and generalise poorly to new
proteins, while classical methods remained competitive \citep{buttenschoen2024posebusters}.

Docking methods are usually compared by redocking: the crystal ligand is removed and docked
back into its protein, and the top-ranked pose is counted as a success if its heavy-atom
RMSD to the crystal pose is below \SI{2}{\angstrom}. Curated sets such as the Astex Diverse
set \citep{hartshorn2007astex} and the PoseBusters Benchmark set
\citep{buttenschoen2024posebusters} make these comparisons repeatable, and PoseBusters adds
checks of chemical and physical plausibility, so that a pose close to the crystal structure
but with clashes or distorted geometry is not counted as a success. Large comparisons of
programs and scoring functions \citep{warren2006critical} have shaped how docking is chosen
and used.

Every such comparison also fixes a preparation protocol: how the protein is protonated and
repaired, which cofactors and waters are kept, which protonation state and starting
conformer the ligand is given, and how the search space is defined. These choices are
usually treated as settled details, yet each is known to matter. Protonation, tautomeric and
stereoisomeric states change docking results \citep{tenbrink2009protonation}; including
water molecules changes them too \citep{roberts2008water}; the size of the search box
affects docking accuracy \citep{feinstein2015boxsize}; and docking can respond chaotically
to small changes in its input \citep{feher2012chaotic}. For whole benchmarks, differences
in how the same dataset is prepared alone produce substantial variation in reported success
rates \citep{corbeil2012variability}, and the protein and ligand preparation protocol
influences virtual screening enrichment \citep{sastry2013preparation}. Guidelines for
evaluating computational methods have long asked that preparation be reported and that
results come with statistical uncertainty \citep{jain2008recommendations}. Even so, it is
rarely measured how large preparation effects are compared with the differences between
engines that benchmarks are designed to detect, or how large they are compared with the
run-to-run variation of a single engine.

Here we measure this directly. DockPrepBench redocks the 428 complexes of the PoseBusters
Benchmark set under a reproducible baseline protocol and under arms that each change one
preparation choice, with four engines: the classical AutoDock Vina, Smina
\citep{koes2013smina} and rDock \citep{ruizcarmona2014rdock}, and Gnina
\citep{mcnutt2021gnina}, which uses the same search as Smina but re-ranks poses with a
convolutional neural network. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a deterministic preparation and docking pipeline in which every arm is evaluated on
    the same complexes, so that arms can be compared complex by complex with exact paired
    tests;
  \item the run-to-run variation of the baseline across docking seeds, which sets the
    smallest effect worth interpreting;
  \item effect sizes for search-box padding, ligand protonation and the ligand start
    conformer, set against the differences between engines;
  \item a failure-mode analysis that separates scoring failures from search failures and
    wrong sites from wrong binding modes, explaining how each choice changes outcomes; and
  \item open code, configurations and per-complex results, with an interactive explorer.
\end{itemize}
