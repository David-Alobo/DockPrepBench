\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The choice of docking engine was the largest single factor in this study, but preparation
was large enough to change how engines compare. Gnina's re-scoring of the same search
placed it 16.4 points above Vina at the baseline protocol, and the four engines spanned
33.2 points, while across the six protocols (baseline and five arms) top-1 success varied
by 7.0 to 10.5 points within each Vina-family engine and by 23.8 points for rDock. Gnina
led under every preparation choice, while Vina and Smina, whose baseline success differed
by less than a point, changed order between arms by at most 1.7 points, a difference no
test here resolves. Among the primary arms, only one choice, the size
of the search box, shifted success beyond what multiple testing allows, and it did so for
Vina, Smina and rDock. The best single choice for Vina closed about 40\% of its gap to Gnina;
combining it with protonation, whose effect was largest in a tight box although the trend
was not established (Section~\ref{sec:interaction}), closed two thirds, and this combined protocol (57.2\%)
came within 1.4 points of Gnina's worst arm (58.6\%), although Gnina itself was not run
with the combination. rDock, an engine outside the Vina family, followed the same pattern
more strongly: its success moved by 23.8 points across the arms, almost all of it through
the box, so for an engine whose search space grows with the box, the box can matter as
much as the choice of engine. For anyone comparing engines, these numbers set a scale: differences
between engines of a few points, measured under a single preparation protocol, are within
the range that preparation alone produces.

Net success rates hide most of what preparation does. For Vina, the one engine with seed
replicates, every arm changed the outcome of more complexes than a change of docking seed
did, up to 4.9 times as many, and most of these changes fell on complexes whose outcome
did not change across four seeds: the regenerated start
conformer changed 63 such complexes while moving the success rate by only 4.4 points,
because gains and losses nearly cancelled. A study that docks a particular ligand series,
or that reports which complexes an engine solves, inherits this sensitivity even when
benchmark-level rates look stable. That docking responds strongly to small changes in its
input has been reported before \citep{feher2012chaotic}; here it is quantified against the
seed-to-seed variation of the same protocol.

The failure-mode analysis shows how the choices act. The search box worked through
sampling, not scoring: a tight box removed nearly all wrong-site poses and raised the
number of complexes with any correct pose, and larger boxes did the reverse for every
engine, sending top poses to high-scoring sites away from the crystal ligand. This agrees
with earlier work showing that box size affects docking accuracy
\citep{feinstein2015boxsize}, and it means that a box drawn generously around a known
ligand is not a neutral default. Gnina was the least sensitive to the box, consistent
with its re-scoring rejecting many of the wrong-site poses that a larger box admits, and
rDock the most, since its cavity grows with the box: at \SI{8}{\angstrom} padding over
half of its top poses lay in the wrong site. The
start conformer, in contrast, changed which basin the search ended in, in both directions,
and its effect was not related to ligand size or flexibility: it behaves like a
reproducible perturbation of a stochastic search rather than a better or worse input.
Protonation at pH 7.4 had small effects that leaned towards the ligands whose charge it
changed, and no effect survived correction among the primary arms; in the follow-up runs
its effect was significant only in combination with the \SI{2}{\angstrom} box.

These results suggest four practices for docking benchmarks and applications. First,
report the search space exactly (centre, size and how it was derived), since it is the
preparation choice with the largest and most consistent effect. Where a reference ligand
is available, a tight box (\SI{2}{\angstrom} padding here) raised success for all four
engines, significantly for Vina, Smina and rDock. Second, repeat runs with several seeds and report the spread: a single run cannot
separate a real difference from seed noise, which here amounted to 17.5 changed complexes
between two runs of the same protocol. Third, compare protocols complex by complex with
paired tests and report how many complexes changed, not only the net rate. Fourth, state
the ligand protonation and start conformer used, as both change individual outcomes even
when they leave the overall rate unchanged.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several choices are fixed in all arms. Cofactors are removed in the baseline because most
lack Meeko templates; trial runs already show top poses placed in a cofactor's site, which
the PoseBusters checks then reject. Waters cannot be studied because the dataset's protein
files contain none. A small number of complexes fail receptor conversion because atoms in
the deposited structure lie close enough for a spurious bond to be perceived; they are not
repaired, since that would move experimental atoms. This affects 16 complexes for Vina
(15 with \SI{2}{\angstrom} padding, where one problem residue falls outside Meeko's
cutoff); they fail in the baseline and in each arm alike, so they barely affect the paired
comparisons, and the other engines, which do not use Meeko, dock them.

The benchmark is redocking into the crystal structure of each complex, with the search
space defined from the crystal ligand. The receptor is held rigid in the conformation it
adopts around that ligand, so induced fit is built into the input rather than tested, and
a preparation step that moved side chains could favour one engine over another in ways
this design cannot show. This is the most favourable setting for docking; in
cross-docking, or when the site must be predicted, both the absolute rates and the size of
preparation effects may differ. Repeating the same arms on a cross-docking benchmark, with
each ligand docked into a structure solved with a different ligand or into an apo or
predicted structure, would test whether these effects hold, or grow, when the receptor was
not shaped by the ligand being docked; the pipeline supports this without change once such
pairs are defined.

The design changes one choice at a time from a common baseline. Each primary arm is crossed
with every engine but not with the other preparation choices, so each effect is measured at
the baseline setting of the others. Only one interaction, protonation with box size, was
tested, for Vina only, and it was suggestive but not established; others, such as the start
conformer with box size, were not tested. A factorial or fractional factorial design over
the preparation choices would capture such interactions at greater computational cost. Each
choice was also tested at a few fixed settings. Ligands were docked in a single protonation
state rather than as an ensemble of likely protomers, tautomers were not enumerated, and
only the ligand side of protonation was varied, the receptor being protonated at pH 7.4
throughout. The box scaled to the ligand's size was tested for Vina only. The seed
replicates that set the noise floor were run for Vina only; Smina and Gnina share much of
Vina's search, but their own run-to-run variation, and rDock's, was not measured. The analysis
of which complexes are sensitive is exploratory, and the properties tested explained
little of the variation. Results cover four engines and one dataset.
